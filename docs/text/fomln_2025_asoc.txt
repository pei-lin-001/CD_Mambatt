ABSTRACT
Accurately predicting the remaining useful life (RUL) of rotating machinery is a challenging task in the field of 
Prognostics and Health Management (PHM). In practical applications, the number of samples in the target 
domain is often insufficient. To address this issue, we propose a First-Order Meta-Learning Network (FOMLN) to 
tackle the problem of equipment RUL prediction under limited samples. First, a meta-learner is constructed based 
on Conformer, combining the advantages of the self-attention mechanism and convolutional neural networks, 
enhancing the model ’ s ability to capture both local and global features. Then, a dual-loop meta-learning strategy 
is designed: the inner loop learns at the sample level, modeling and updating parameters for specific tasks, while 
the outer loop updates the meta-parameters through task-level learning, improving the model ’ s generalization 
across different tasks and its adaptability to new tasks under limited sample conditions. Extensive experimental 
results on the C-MAPSS dataset validate the effectiveness of the proposed method. Moreover, a practical 
application case study is introduced, demonstrating the model ’ s ability to predict the RUL of slurry pumps in an 
industrial site under few-shot scenarios, highlighting its potential for real-world applications.
1. Introduction
In the field of prognostic health management (PHM), accurately 
estimating the remaining useful life (RUL) of machinery is a critical task 
[1] , serving as the foundation for predictive maintenance and opera -
tional decision-making. The primary objective of RUL prediction is to 
forecast the potential failure time of equipment, thus enabling pre -
emptive maintenance and enhancing reliability. In recent years, this 
topic has garnered considerable attention from researchers, leading to 
substantial advancements [2] .
With the progress in information technology, machine learning (ML) 
techniques have gained prominence in RUL prediction [3] . Notably, 
methods based on convolutional neural networks (CNN), Transformer 
models [4] , and recurrent neural networks (RNN) such as long 
short-term memory (LSTM) networks [5] and gated recurrent units 
(GRU) [6] have shown promise. These ML approaches excel at auto -
matically extracting features from complex data and handling 
high-dimensional, nonlinear datasets [7] . For instance, Hu et al. [8]
integrated CNN with quantile regression to quantify the uncertainty in 
RUL predictions. Similarly, Liang et al. [9] introduced a Bayesian 
framework into GRU to improve the accuracy of RUL estimation.
While ML-based RUL prediction methods have made considerable 
progress, they still face serious challenges in real-world industrial 
deployment [10] . Firstly, these models struggle to effectively capture

Existing approaches often exhibit trade-offs: RNN-like models insuffi -
ciently model long-range dependencies, CNNs have difficulty incorpo -
rating global context, and Transformers are less efficient in extracting 
local features. Therefore, designing a method that can extract and fuse 
both local and global features from raw sensor data to construct a 
comprehensive feature representation highly predictive of RUL is a 
crucial bottleneck for improving the performance of current RUL pre -
diction models.
The second challenge lies in the domain shift problem [11,12] , 
particularly when a model is trained in one environment and deployed 
in a different one. Domain shift can be caused by a variety of factors, 
such as different operating conditions, equipment wear status, or envi -
ronmental changes [13] . This phenomenon poses a challenge to the 
accuracy of RUL prediction, as the model may not be able to effectively 
generalize from the training data (source domain) to the actual appli -
cation environment (target domain) [14] . To address the domain shift 
problem, domain adaptation (DA) technology has been proposed. DA 
methods usually require a large number of unlabeled target domain 
samples to adjust the model so that it can better generalize to new en -
vironments [15,16] . For example, Zou et al. [17] proposed a dynamic 
benchmark (DB)-based transfer prediction model to predict the RUL of 
bearings, using autoencoders and DB-based domain adaptation methods 
to reconstruct features. However, the scenarios in real applications are 
often more demanding, because obtaining a large number of target 
domain samples is time-consuming and expensive, especially in indus -
trial applications of high-value equipment [18] . Therefore, how to 
achieve effective cross-domain learning in situations where the number 
of target domain samples is severely limited has become a critical 
problem in the RUL prediction field. Although meta-learning is a po -
tential solution to this problem [19] , most meta-learning models in the 
RUL prediction domain are implemented based on the Model-Agnostic 
Meta-Learning (MAML) [20] framework. While MAML has strong 
adaptability and theoretical foundations, its training process involves 
the calculation of second-order gradients of the loss function, which 
leads to high computational complexity and resource consumption, 
making it difficult to apply effectively in resource-constrained 
environments.
To address the limitations outlined above – namely, the insufficient 
multi-scale feature representation in existing RUL prediction models, the 
scarcity of target-domain samples in domain-shift scenarios, and the 
computational drawbacks of current MAML-based meta-learning ap -
proaches – this paper proposes a First-Order Meta-Learning Network 
(FOMLN). The method is based on first-order meta-learning framework 
[21] . The inner loop updates task-level parameters for a specific 
meta-task by learning at the level of individual samples. The outer loop, 
in turn, updates meta-level parameters by aligning the model’s param -
eters with aggregated, optimal parameters derived from each meta-task. 
Moreover, to facilitate effective extraction of multi-scale degradation 
features, we developed a meta-learner based on a Conformer [22] ar -
chitecture. This meta-learner effectively merges the local feature 
extraction strengths of CNNs with the aptitude of Transformers for 
processing long-range sequence dependencies. By integrating this 
meta-learner into the FOMLN framework, our model is capable of 
learning robust and generalizable feature representations, even from 
limited data, enabling rapid adaptation to novel, data-scarce target 
domains.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows: 
1. An efficient first-order episodic meta-training strategy for FOMLN is 
proposed. This strategy relies on the first-order derivative of the loss 
function to perform inner (task-level parameter update) and outer 
(meta-level parameter update) loop optimization, reducing compu -
tational complexity. This enables FOMLN to be effectively trained 
with limited samples and rapidly adapted to new RUL prediction 
tasks with only a small number of target samples.
2. A Conformer-based meta-learner has been constructed and inte -
grated into the FOMLN framework. By combining the local percep -
tion capability of CNNs and the global modeling capability of 
Transformers, this learner effectively addresses the challenge of 
extracting multi-scale features from device degradation signals.
3. Extensive experimental validation on the public benchmark dataset 
C-MAPSS, particularly in the few-shot cross-domain RUL prediction 
setting that has received less attention in previous studies, demon -
strates the superiority of the proposed method. Furthermore, a case 
study predicting the RUL of a slurry pump in an industrial field 
environment further showcases the practical application potential 
and value of FOMLN in real-world few-shot scenarios.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 includes 
related work on the method, Section 3 presents the overall structure and 
training method of the model, and related experiments are conducted in 
Section 4 . Finally, Section 5 summarizes the entire paper.
2. Related work
2.1. Multi-scale learning-based RUL prediction
Multi-scale learning in RUL prediction aims to achieve a more 
comprehensive feature representation by extracting representative 
degradation features at different temporal or spatial scales. For instance, 
Xu et al. [23] extracted multi-scale features from raw sensor signals 
using a multi-scale temporal convolutional network and combined them 
with an attention mechanism to predict the RUL of aero-engines. Guo 
et al. [24] addressed the challenges of capturing multi-scale degradation 
trends and extracting long-term dependencies in aero-engine RUL pre -
diction by combining a multi-scale feature extractor and a Transformer 
network, constructing an Hourglass-Transformer model (MHT). They 
validated the method’s superiority and generalizability on the C-MAPSS 
and N-CMAPSS datasets. Zhou et al. [25] solved the problem of sepa -
rating RUL prediction and Health State (HS) assessment tasks in 
aero-engines, as well as the limitations of single-scale feature extraction, 
by combining multi-scale feature fusion and multi-task joint learning, 
constructing a multi-task convolutional neural network (MSMT-CNN). 
Li et al. [26] constructed a Dual-Scale Transformer model (DSFormer) 
by combining a dual-attention mechanism and a temporal convolutional 
network (TCN), addressing the impact of sensor differences and the loss 
of location information in multivariate time-series RUL prediction in the 
Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) environment. While the above 
studies have all validated the effectiveness of their multi-scale feature 
extraction methods on public benchmark datasets such as C-MAPSS, 
these works have largely been achieved under relatively data-rich con -
ditions. In real-world industrial scenarios, degradation data, especially 
near the failure stage of equipment’s life cycle, is often very scarce. This

2.2. Meta-learning and few-shot learning-based RUL prediction
Meta-learning [27] , or the concept of "learning how to learn," focuses 
on enabling models to rapidly adapt to new tasks, even when only a 
small number of data points are available for the model to adapt to. 
Therefore, meta-learning is an important approach to solving few-shot 
learning (FSL) problems. These methods have shown promise in fields 
such as medical imaging and natural language processing [28] . As 
illustrated in Fig. 1 , meta-learning differs from traditional ML by 
enabling models to learn to quickly adapt to new tasks, allowing them to 
achieve effective generalization with limited target domain data [27] . 
This is particularly valuable in data-scarce environments; therefore,

et al. [31] combined transfer learning techniques with meta-learning for 
rocket engine RUL prediction tasks under few-shot scenarios; Yang et al. 
[32] proposed a Meta-Learning with Deep Flow Kernel Network model

verified the effectiveness of the proposed method on degradation data -

While the above MAML-based meta-learning methods demonstrate 
good performance in few-shot cross-domain RUL prediction tasks, these 
approaches require the calculation of second-order derivatives with 
respect to model parameters during the meta-training phase, resulting in 
extremely time-consuming training processes. Furthermore, the second- 
order derivative calculation itself has poor numerical stability and is 
prone to gradient explosion or vanishing problems, which can affect the 
model ’ s convergence speed and final performance.
3. Methodology
The overall process of the proposed FOMLN is shown in Fig. 2 . Data is 
collected from the monitored equipment to form a training set and a test 
set, where the training set is divided into multiple meta-tasks and the 
test set is treated as a separate test task. For each meta-task in the 
training set, FOMLN replicates the structure and parameters of its meta- 
learner to generate a base learner and performs gradient updates on the 
meta-task through the structure of the encoder and regressor to model 
the meta-task at the sample level. Then, the first-order episodic meta- 
training strategy is designed to align the gradient directions of each 
base learner, enabling meta-level parameter updates on the meta- 
learner and facilitating task-level learning. In the testing phase, a pre -
dictor that shares initial parameters and structure with the meta-learner 
is generated based on the test task to perform RUL prediction on the test 
set. This approach allows FOMLN to learn common knowledge across 
different tasks, enabling rapid adaptation when confronted with new 
test tasks.
3.1. Construction of Meta-Learner
ML models used for RUL prediction usually consist of an encoder and 
a predictor. In the construction of the model encoder, the attention 
mechanism has become a hot topic in recent years because it can capture 
long-distance dependencies, which is very important when processing 
complex time series data. However, the attention mechanism usually 
requires a lot of computing resources and is not as effective as CNN when 
processing high-frequency data. On the other hand, although CNN 
performs well in extracting local features and processing high-frequency 
data, it is not as powerful as the attention mechanism in capturing long- 
term dependencies. In order to combine the advantages of both, the 
Conformer [22] was proposed. By combining the advantages of the 
attention mechanism and convolution, it can not only effectively capture 
long-term dependencies in time series data, but also process complex 
frequency domain features [35] . This combination makes the Conformer 
both efficient and reliable when processing complex industrial data, but 
to the best of our knowledge, no scholars have yet explored the per -
formance of this architecture in RUL prediction tasks.
In summary, we designed the meta-learner based on Conformer in 
Fig. 2 , which consists of an encoder and a regressor. The encoder part 
consists of a one-dimensional convolutional layer and a sequence of N 
Conformer blocks to perform feature extraction on the input data. These 
features are then passed to the regressor, whose purpose is to map the 
high-dimensional feature space into the device ’ s RUL. The linear module 
in the regressor consists of a flattening operation followed by a fully 
connected layer. The flattening operation adjusts the dimension of the 
encoder output to make it suitable for the fully connected layer, which is 
used to learn and predict the target RUL.
The encoder of the meta-learner consists of a 1D convolution and N 
Conformer Blocks, where the 1D convolution transforms the input data 
into a higher-level feature representation and adjusts its dimension for 
subsequent feature extraction by the conformer block. In the conformer 
block, the data after the 1D convolution next enters the Feed Forward 
Module (FFM) to obtain additional representation capabilities; it then 
enters the multi-head self-attention module to capture the mechanism of 
complex interactions between different positions in the sequence. It does 
this by learning information represented by different subspaces in the 
sequence in parallel. A self-attention layer allows each element in the 
input sequence to pay attention to (or weight) all other elements in the 
sequence to better encode the element. In the model proposed in this 
article, self-attention is calculated through the following steps [36] :

3.2. Adaptive weighted loss function
In actual operation, equipment is in health state for most of the time, 
and only shows degradation trends when it is close to failure. Therefore, 
the number of samples with a relatively long RUL or health state is 
relatively large. This leads to an imbalanced distribution of labels in the 
dataset when using equipment degradation data to train neural net -
works, causing the model ’ s prediction results to be biased towards the 
majority class in the data [37] , that is, samples with longer RUL. This 
situation will cause the model ’ s performance to deteriorate when pre -
dicting shorter RUL, because the model does not "see" such samples 
enough during the training process [38] .
This label imbalance problem is particularly significant in few-shot 
scenarios. In the meta-learning process, the number of samples in each 
meta-task is extremely small, which makes the label imbalance problem 
more prominent than in traditional machine learning models. In this 
case, the model is more susceptible to label imbalance because the 
proportion of minority class labels present in a small number of samples 
is further reduced. This causes the model to learn frequently occurring 
situations most of the time and ignore all situations, resulting in an 
inefficient learning process and poor performance when encountering 
minority class samples.
To address the problem of label imbalance in training data, we 
propose an adaptive weighted loss function so that different RUL sam -
ples in the same meta-batch contribute differently to the loss. This 
weighted loss function usually gives higher weight to minority samples 
(samples with shorter RUL) and lower weight to majority samples 
(samples with longer RUL). This design balances the impact of a few 
labels on model training by dynamically adjusting weights in the in -
ternal and external loops of meta-learning. This loss can be expressed as:

tasks. Based on this idea, we design a first order episodic meta training

capture key degradation information, while CS overcomes this short -
coming. The eigenvalues after CS processing have an obvious degrada -
tion trend and thus have better discrimination and prediction 
capabilities.

4.1.4. Few-shot cross-domain prediction
Compared to domain invariant tasks, few-shot cross-domain tasks 
are more challenging because the model must learn to extract knowl -
edge from one domain ’ s data and apply it to a different distribution in 
another domain. This requires the model not only to capture important 
features within the domain but also to possess a certain degree of 
domain invariance to adapt to the data distribution of the new domain. 
Tables 6 and 7 show the results of the few-shot cross-domain tasks 
compared with published works. In the tables, Source represents the 
source domain dataset, Target represents the target domain dataset, the 
model is trained on the source domain, and tested on the target domain ’ s 
test set. From the tables, we can see that: (1) The proposed method 
demonstrates excellent performance in multiple tasks. Particularly in 
few-shot cross-domain tasks such as FD004 to FD001 and FD002 to 
FD001, it outperforms other methods. The proposed method achieves 
the lowest RMSE values in all scenarios, indicating its strong predictive 
ability in few-shot cross-domain learning scenarios. (2) Although LSTM- 
DANN [16] , DIDRLSTM [51] , CADA [52] and VLSTM-LWSAN [53]
methods have excellent performance in multiple tasks and achieved 
lower SCORE, they use a large number of unlabeled samples in the target 
domain for domain adaptation, which means that they have more 
information to learn and adapt, which may be a limitation in practical 
applications because a large number of unlabeled samples in the target 
domain may be difficult to obtain. The proposed method was trained 
and domain adapted in a 15-shot scenario, that is, FOMLN only used 15 
samples in the target domain and achieved performance similar to or 
even better than the SOTA model based on transfer learning, which 
shows the superiority of meta-learning-based models in the face of 
domain shift problems in the context of scarce samples. (3) Among 
meta-learning-based models, FOMLN ’ performance is better than 
MAML-based model [30] . This demonstrates that the proposed method 
also has advantages when compared with models of the same type.
4.2. Case study 2: RUL prediction using real-world data
4.2.1. Experimental design
To establish the practical application value of the proposed method 
in this study, we conducted experiments on few-shot RUL prediction of 
slurry pumps at an industrial site. Due to confidentiality agreements, 
actual images of the industrial site slurry pumps cannot be disclosed; 
therefore, schematic diagrams are used to represent the equipment used 
in the experiments, as shown in Fig. 8 . At the experimental site, we 
monitored the operation data of the slurry pump under two working 
conditions, each consistent with the information listed in Table 8 .
During the experiment, a vibration sensor was installed on the non- 
drive end of the pump to collect vibration signals throughout the pump ’ s 
entire operational life. The sensor had a sampling frequency of 
12,800 Hz, and data was recorded every half hour. Each recording lasted 
2.56 s, resulting in 32,768 data points per recording (12,800 Hz ×
2.56 s). Over the pump ’ s operational lifetime, exceeding 1200 h for each 
condition, approximately 2400 raw time-series recordings were 
collected. The operating conditions listed in Table 8 were maintained 
relatively stable during each phase. However, minor fluctuations 
inherent to the industrial process were present. The ground truth RUL 
labels was determined by the maintenance log. Specifically, it was 
defined by the point in time when the pump was shut down due to 
significantly degraded performance attributed to casing wear. This 
casing wear was confirmed by subsequent visual inspection (as shown in 
Fig. 9 ), which revealed severe wear due to mineral fragment impact, the 
transport of corrosive media, and variations in pump operating 
conditions.

tions, which are attributed to the continuous performance degradation 
caused by the harsh operating conditions of the slurry pump. Despite the 
severe fluctuations, most features show an overall upward trend over 
time, indicating that the equipment has been in a state of degradation 
since the beginning of its operation. Based on this observation, there is 
no need to use piecewise functions to describe the health state when 
setting RUL labels for the samples in this study. We employed the same 
data normalization method as in Case Study 1 and utilized a sliding 
window technique to expand the sample dimensions to (15, 30), in order 
to align with the input format of the model.
For comparison, we also constructed several benchmark models, 
including a Deep Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN) and a Trans -
former, structure of the DCNN is consistent with the one described in 
[54] . It processes the 2D input (time window × features) using primarily 
1D convolutions along the time dimension. Four initial convolutional 
layers (10 filters each, kernel size 10 × 1, tanh activation, zero-padding) 
are stacked, followed by a fifth convolutional layer (1 filter, kernel size 
3 × 1, tanh activation) to combine the preceding feature maps. The 
resulting feature map is then flattened, passed through dropout with a 
rate of 0.5, followed by a fully connected layer (100 neurons, tanh 
activation), and finally connected to a single linear output neuron for 
RUL prediction. The Transformer baseline employed an encoder-only 
architecture follows [36] , specifically adapted for the time-series 
regression task. It consisted of 2 stacked Transformer encoder layers 
Transformer encoder ’ s structure, with the number of heads set to 4, the 
dimension set to 128, a dropout rate of 0.1, and 2 layers, using a linear 
layer as the regressor.

proposed method.

4.2.3. Experimental results and analysis
Fig. 11 illustrates the match between the predicted RUL and the 
actual RUL for each model in the 10-shot learning cross-domain task. 
From the Fig. 11 , it is evident that few-shot cross-domain RUL prediction 
in real industrial environments is more challenging compared to Case 
Study 1. In industrial scenarios, the monitored equipment ’ s condition 
changes continuously with production tasks, and there is more noise and 
interference present in the industrial site, making the collected data less 
ideal than laboratory data. Fig. 11 shows that all models ’ predictions 
exhibit some degree of fluctuation. Notably, models using DCNN as the 
encoder tend to predict the RUL as the average of the total RUL 
(approximately 600 h). This is because convolution-based models can 
only focus on local information while ignoring global information, 
leading to a near-total loss of capability to capture degradation infor -
mation of the equipment in complex and challenging industrial settings. 
Consequently, these models resort to predicting an average value to 
minimize training loss.
In contrast, FOMLN ’ s performance stands out, with its predicted 
values closely aligning with the actual RUL values during the latter 
stages of operation, highlighting its ability to grasp task-relevant infor -
mation and adapt to new environments. The RMSE comparison in 
Fig. 12 further underscores FOMLN ’ s superiority: it achieved the lowest 
RMSE value of 102.03 among all models, reflecting its prediction ac -
curacy. This performance demonstrates FOMLN ’ s adaptability and pre -
diction accuracy in handling few-shot cross-domain tasks within 
complex industrial environments. It showcases the model ’ s accuracy 
and reliability in capturing equipment degradation information, 
emphasizing its practical applicability in real industrial settings.
Furthermore, to empirically evaluate the stability and robustness of 
the proposed FOMLN framework, we conducted 10 repeated experi -
ments. Fig. 13 presents the distribution of the final RMSE results across 
these 10 runs using violin plots. As clearly depicted, FOMLN not only 
achieves the lowest average RMSE compared to all baseline methods but 
also exhibits the most concentrated distribution. The violin plot for 
FOMLN is notably narrower, and its standard deviation is considerably 
smaller than those of the competing models, especially compared to the 
DCNN-based approaches which show wide variability.
4.3. Ablation study
To better explore the impact of each component of the proposed 
FOMLN on the overall model performance, we designed several ablation 
experiments to systematically evaluate the contribution of each part of 
the model.
4.3.1. Meta-learner architecture
In this experiment, we constructed different meta-learner 
architectures and used the same meta-learning strategy for training and 
testing to discuss the effectiveness of the Conformer-based meta-learner 
in improving overall model performance. For comparison, we con -
structed a CNN-based meta-learner (referred to as Meta-Learner 1, with 
the structure consistent with [54] ), a self-attention mechanism-based 
meta-learner (referred to as Meta-Learner 2, with the structure consis -
tent with [36] ), and the proposed meta-learner that combines the ad -
vantages of both. Experiments were conducted on four datasets under 
the same few-shot scenarios. The RMSE results are shown in Fig. 14 , and 
the SCORE results are shown in Fig. 15 .
From the figures, it can be observed that the proposed meta-learner 
achieved the lowest RMSE and SCORE values across all datasets, fully 
demonstrating the effectiveness of combining convolutional neural 
networks and self-attention mechanisms in few-shot RUL prediction 
tasks. By integrating both architectures, we achieved superior perfor -
mance compared to using a single architecture, validating the superi -
ority and applicability of this method in complex tasks.
4.3.2. Adaptive weighted loss
To investigate the impact of the proposed adaptive weighted loss 
function on the performance of the proposed method in few-shot sce -
narios, we constructed a model using the standard MSE loss function, 
referred to as Baseline. Except for the loss function, the structure and 
hyperparameter configuration of this model are identical to FOMLN. We 
conducted experiments on four datasets in case study 1 to test their 
performance. Figs. 16 and 17 respectively present the comparison of 
their RMSE and SCORE values.
From the figures, it can be observed that on the simpler FD001 and 
FD003 datasets, the performance difference between the proposed 
method and the Baseline is minimal. However, on the more variable 
FD002 and FD004 datasets, the proposed method significantly reduces 
the SCORE. Although the RMSE indicates that the average prediction 
error of both methods is relatively close, the Baseline tends to predict 
higher RUL values due to the higher frequency of high RUL samples seen 
during training, resulting in a higher SCORE. This outcome demon -
strates the clear advantage of the adaptive weighted loss function in 
handling label imbalance, particularly in complex conditions and few- 
shot scenarios, where it can enhance the model ’ s prediction accuracy 
and robustness.

learning rate η , the number of inner loop steps k , and the adaptive 
weighted loss tuning exponent β on the model ’ s performance, aiming to 
provide insights into hyperparameter selection and their influence on

The sensitivity analysis was performed on the challenging cross- 
domain task FD004 to FD001 under the 15-shot scenario, as this sce -
nario involves complex operating conditions, multiple fault modes, and 
domain shift, making it representative for evaluating robustness. For 
each parameter tested, others were kept at their default values reported 
in Section 4 ( α = 0 . 1 , η = 0 . 001 , k = 10 , β = 1), unless otherwise 
specified. Performance was evaluated using RMSE.
4.4.1. Learning rates α and η
The dual-loop learning rates, α (outer) and η (inner), are crucial for 
balancing meta-level generalization and task-specific adaptation. We 
varied α within the range [0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5] while keeping η =
0 . 001 and k = 10. Subsequently, we varied η within [0.0001, 0.0005, 
0.001, 0.005, 0.01] while keeping α = 0 . 1 and k = 10. The RMSE results 
are shown in Figs. 18 and 19 .
As shown in Figs. 18 and 19 , a very small α (e.g., 0.01) resulted in 
slow convergence of the meta-parameters, leading to suboptimal per -
formance within the fixed number of outer loop epochs. Conversely, a 
large α (e.g., 0.5) caused instability in the meta-update, where the meta- 
parameters oscillated and failed to converge to a good initialization, 
resulting in poor performance. The value α = 0.1 provided a good 
balance, enabling stable and efficient convergence towards a robust 
meta-initialization. And the inner loop learning rate dictates the speed 
and extent of adaptation within each task. A very small η (e.g., 0.0001) 
led to insufficient adaptation within the k = 10 steps, meaning the base 
learners did not adequately fit their respective tasks, hindering the 
quality of the meta-gradient estimate. A large η (e.g., 0.01) caused the 
inner loop optimization to be unstable or overshoot the optimal pa -
rameters for the task, also degrading meta-learning performance. The 
value η = 0.001 allowed for effective task adaptation within the inner 
loop without causing instability.
4.4.2. Number of inner loop steps k
The number of inner loop gradient descent steps, k , determines how 
much adaptation occurs for each task before the meta-update. We varied 
k in the range [1,5,10,15,20] while keeping α = 0.1 and η = 0.001. Re -
sults are shown in Fig. 20 .
Fig. 20 indicates that performance generally improves as k increases 
from 1 to 10. With very few steps ( k = 1 or k = 5), the base learners cannot 
sufficiently adapt to their specific tasks, resulting poor meta-update 
quality. As k increases to 10, the adaptation becomes more refined, 
leading to better meta-learning. However, increasing k further ( k = 15 or 
k = 20) showed diminishing returns and even a slight increase in RMSE. 
This could be attributed to potential overfitting within the inner loop to 
the small number of samples (15-shot) in each task, which might slightly 
harm the generalization ability captured by the meta-parameters. 
Additionally, larger k greatly increases the computational cost of 
training. Therefore, k = 10 was selected as it offered the best balance 
between adaptation quality, generalization performance, and compu -
tational efficiency.
4.4.3. Adaptive loss tuning exponent β
The adaptive weighted loss function includes a tuning exponent β 
that controls the strength of the weighting applied to address label 
imbalance. We investigated the impact of varying β in the range [0, 0.5, 
1.0, 1.5, 2.0], where β = 0 corresponds to the standard MSE loss 
(equivalent to the Baseline model in the ablation study).
The RMSE results are shown in Fig. 21 , showing that setting β 
= 0 (standard MSE) results in the highest RMSE, as standard MSE has 
difficulty coping with unbalanced RUL values. As β increases to 0.5 and 
1.0, the model performance improves. The best performance is achieved 
around β = 1.0. Further increasing β to 1.5 and 2.0 results in a slight 
decrease in performance. This suggests that too high a weighting (high 
β ) may cause the model to be overly sensitive to minority class samples 
or potential noise therein, especially in the few-shot scenario. Therefore, 
we choose β = 1 as the optimal value.
5. Conclusion
This study proposes an innovative meta-learning model — FOMLN, 
specifically designed for RUL prediction tasks of rotating machinery in 
limited sample scenarios. FOMLN ’ s meta-learner integrates convolution 
and self-attention mechanisms within the Conformer architecture. Using 
a first-order episodic meta-learning strategy, it performs dual-loop 
learning at multiple levels: the inner loop conducts fine-grained 
learning at the sample level, capturing task-specific details and updat -
ing parameters; the outer loop operates at the task level, gradually 
aligning the model parameters with the optimal parameter set for each 
meta-task. This approach enhances the model ’ s generalization capa -
bility across tasks and its rapid adaptation to new tasks in few-shot 
scenarios.
Extensive experiments were conducted on various datasets to eval -
uate the performance of the proposed method. Detailed experiments 
using the C-MAPSS dataset confirmed the effectiveness of FOMLN in FSL 
scenarios. The results demonstrated not only FOMLN ’ s superior per -
formance across single fault modes and different operating conditions 
but also highlighted its enhanced prediction accuracy and reduced 
reliance on target domain sample sizes in few-shot cross-domain 
learning tasks, compared to other state-of-the-art models. In a critical 
industrial application involving the prediction of the RUL of slurry 
pumps in FSL scenarios, FOMLN greatly outperformed other benchmark 
models, including traditional transfer learning and meta-learning 
approaches.
