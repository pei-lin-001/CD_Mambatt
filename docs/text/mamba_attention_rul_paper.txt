 A B S T R A C T
Accurate remaining useful life (RUL) prediction provides significant economic, technical, and safety benefits 
by forecasting the lifespans of industrial equipment. Current state-of-the-art (SOTA) RUL prediction models 
predominantly rely on recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and attention-based models. While RNNs are adept at 
capturing temporal dependencies, they struggle with issues such as vanishing gradients and exhibit inefficiency 
in processing long sequences. Meanwhile, attention-based models improve the capture of global context 
but are limited by their high computational complexity. The proposed Mamba-Attention model addresses 
these limitations by integrating a selective state space model with attention mechanisms, achieving SOTA 
performance with faster training times on C-MAPSS dataset. Industrial equipment is frequently replaced 
prematurely, and obtaining labeled data is challenging due to cost and time constraints. To overcome these 
difficulties, this research introduces a novel framework that leverages unlabeled data via self-supervised 
learning approaches, including temporal loss, N-tuplet loss, and pseudo-label loss. By pre-training with self-
supervised losses and fine-tuning on minimal labeled data in few-shot scenarios, the proposed approach remains 
effective in producing accurate RUL predictions, even under limited supervision, as demonstrated on the C-
MAPSS and CALCE datasets. This reduces the reliance on costly labeled datasets and offers practical solutions 
for industrial applications.
1. Introduction
Prognostics and health management (PHM) is essential for ensuring 
the reliability of safety-critical industrial systems, such as nuclear plants 
and oil facilities [1]. A key aspect of PHM is remaining useful life 
(RUL) prediction, which provides timely maintenance alerts, reducing 
costs and preventing unexpected failures [2,3]. With the advent of 
industry digitalization and IoT, data-rich environments have enabled 
sophisticated data-driven methods for RUL estimation [2].
RUL prediction methods generally fall into physical model-based, 
knowledge-based, and data-driven approaches [4]. Physical models 
simulate equipment degradation but struggle with complex scenar-
ios [5]. Knowledge-based methods depend heavily on domain expert 
experience and lack generalizability [6]. Data-driven methods, how-
ever, offer superior flexibility and generalization by directly learning 
degradation patterns from sensor data using machine learning [7,8].
Deep learning models, including multi-layer perceptrons (MLP) [9], 
recurrent neural networks (RNN) [10–12], convolutional neural net-
works (CNN) [9], and attention-based models [13,14], have been de-
veloped for improved RUL prediction. Nevertheless, RNNs face van-
ishing gradient issues, and attention-based models suffer from high

these challenges, our research introduces the Mamba architecture [15], 
which efficiently models long-range dependencies without the gradient

Moreover, supervised deep learning models require extensive la-

self-supervised learning, successful in NLP and image domains, has 
potential for RUL tasks but remains underexplored due to the tempo-

This study proposes a novel framework that integrates three self-
supervised losses — temporal loss, N-tuplet loss, and pseudo-label 
loss — to address the challenge of RUL prediction in limited-label 
scenarios. The encoder is first pre-trained using only unlabeled sensor 
data, followed by supervised fine-tuning with minimal labeled data, 
demonstrating strong predictive performance even in one-shot learning 
setups.

We adopt the standard zero-life labeling convention used in prior 
RUL prediction literature [10], where the failure point is labeled as 
RUL = 0. In our framework, ‘‘one-shot’’ refers to using a single labeled 
sample (i.e., one engine for C-MAPSS or one battery for CALCE) to 
train the final prediction head, while the encoder remains frozen. This 
approach enables the model to generalize RUL predictions across differ-
ent units using extremely limited supervision, making it well-suited for 
real-world industrial settings where labeled degradation trajectories are 
often scarce. Furthermore, the framework naturally extends to few-shot 
scenarios, maintaining label efficiency while improving robustness.
Key contributions of this work include:
• Achieving state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance in RUL prediction 
by addressing the limitations of RNNs and attention-based models 
through the use of the MambaAtt architecture.
• Applying self-supervised learning to RUL tasks using a combina-
tion of temporal pattern recognition (temporal loss), contrastive 
learning (N-tuplet loss), and pseudo-labeling (pseudo-label Loss), 
thereby improving model robustness even with limited labeled 
data.
• Demonstrating the effectiveness of few-shot learning in RUL pre-
diction, showing that the proposed method can achieve competi-
tive results comparable to models that do not use self-supervised 
learning losses with minimal labeled data.
2. Related works
2.1. RNN-based approaches
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), including variants like LSTM
[16] and GRU [17], excel at capturing temporal dependencies in se-
quential data. GRU, in particular, has shown effectiveness in RUL pre-
diction by reducing computational complexity and training time [18]. 
Bi-directional GRUs (Bi-GRU) further enhance temporal dependency 
modeling, addressing limitations of unidirectional GRUs [19]. How-
ever, RNN models generally struggle with long-term dependencies, 
motivating research into attention-based approaches.
2.2. Attention-based approaches
The Transformer [20] significantly advanced sequence modeling 
by effectively capturing long-range dependencies. Mo et al. (2021) 
applied a Transformer with gated convolutional units for industrial 
RUL prediction [14]. However, Transformers face quadratic computa-
tional complexity and lack sequential structural modeling. To mitigate 
these issues, Chang et al. (2022) introduced a temporal flow Trans-
former [21], while Shi et al. (2021) combined LSTM and attention 
mechanisms to better capture sequential degradation patterns [22].
2.3. Mamba architecture
Mamba is a selective state space model that dynamically con-
trols information flow across sequences, enabling efficient handling 
of long-range dependencies while discarding irrelevant signals [15]. 
This design overcomes key limitations of RNNs (e.g., vanishing gra-
dients, limited memory) and Transformers (e.g., quadratic complexity 
in sequence length), achieving improved scalability and performance. 
Originally developed for tasks such as language modeling, vision, and 
time series analysis [23], Mamba has recently been applied to battery 
health estimation, including SOH, SOC, and RUL prediction for lithium-
ion batteries [24,25], demonstrating its versatility in prognostics. To 
the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first application 
of a hybrid Mamba-attention architecture for RUL prediction on the 
C-MAPSS dataset.
2.4. Self-supervised learning in RUL prediction
Self-supervised learning leverages the data itself to generate su-
pervisory signals, allowing models to learn valuable representations 
without requiring labeled data. This approach is particularly beneficial 
in the estimation of RUL, where labeled data can be scarce. Self-
supervised learning methods are typically divided into contrastive and 
generative approaches [26]. Contrastive methods focus on training 
an encoder to produce explicit vector representations, measuring the 
similarity between these representations to learn meaningful features. 
Recent studies have applied contrastive methods to RUL prediction. 
Zhu et al. (2024) introduce the Contrastive Bi-LSTM enabled health 
representation learning (CBHRL) framework for RUL prediction, uti-
lizing supervised contrastive learning to achieve superior performance 
over existing methods [27]. However, this approach is not fully self-
supervised, indicating potential for future research in semi-supervised 
or fully self-supervised learning paradigms. Similarly, Yu et al. (2023) 
proposes a self-supervised learning approach built upon a variational 
autoencoder (VAE) for feature extraction in RUL prediction, clustering 
the extracted features to mitigate the negative effect of varying working 
conditions and fault modes on RUL prediction accuracy [28]. However, 
this method relies on self-supervised learning mainly for feature extrac-
tion, with the final task of RUL prediction performed using supervised 
learning on clustered data. In contrast, our approach aims to achieve 
one-shot or few-shot RUL predictions using minimal labeled data. Wang 
et al. (2023) propose a self-supervised health representation decompo-
sition learning (SHRDL) framework, which employs data augmentation 
and a contrastive loss modified by cycle information, based on a mod-
ified Info-NCE, to extract robust health representations [29]. Although 
this study improves RUL prediction, it still depends on supervised learn-
ing after downstream tasks after fine-tuning, and it does not experiment 
with one-shot or few-shot learning scenarios, an area where our study 
focuses on enabling efficient RUL prediction with limited labeled data.
3. Methods
3.1. Overall framework
The proposed framework comprises three main components: a 
Mamba encoder, a Transformer decoder, and a self-supervised training 
strategy. The overall architecture is illustrated in Fig.  1, while the 
training procedure is detailed in Fig.  3. The complete process is also 
summarized in Algorithm 1.
The first part is the Mamba block, which extracts features using 
Mamba, integrating the advantages of both RNNs and Transformers. 
Mamba effectively captures global context and handles long-term de-
pendencies better than RNNs, making it advantageous for processing 
complex sequence data. Additionally, it maximizes computational effi-
ciency by reducing the quadratic computational complexity of Trans-
formers to linear time complexity.
The second part uses the Transformer block to make accurate pre-
dictions from the feature embeddings. The Transformer block leverages 
multi-head attention to focus on various parts of the input sequence, 
effectively managing long-term dependencies and enabling parallel 
processing, which makes it highly efficient for sequence generation 
tasks.
The final component is the self-supervised training strategy. Unlike 
existing RUL self-supervised learning methods that rely solely on con-
trastive learning, our work incorporates a novel loss function designed 
to learn sequential dependencies, thereby enabling the extraction of 
more temporal and contextually relevant features.
3.2. Mamba block
Mamba addresses the limitations of both RNNs and Transformer 
models, offering a scalable and efficient alternative that overcomes the 
computational and memory inefficiencies often encountered in other 
models.

MambAtt employs the Mamba model as an encoder. The Mamba model

Unlike RNNs, this linear model structure enables efficient parallel com-
putation, resulting in faster processing while still capturing sequential 
dependencies.
The structured state space (S4) model [32] builds on SSM by inte-
grating the HiPPO [33] to tackle long-range dependency challenges. 
Since ̄𝐴 captures only the immediate past state, HiPPO effectively 
compresses continuous representations into finite dimensions, enabling 
S4 to model extended dependencies efficiently.
3.2.2. Selective state space model and mamba block
As shown in Fig.  2, the selective State Space Model enhances 
sequence modeling by dynamically adapting to input content, address-
ing the limitations of traditional SSMs that use static, time-invariant 
parameters 𝐴, 𝐵, and 𝐶. By making the matrices 𝐵, 𝐶, and even the step 
size 𝛥 input-dependent, the model can selectively retain and prioritize 
relevant information based on context. To further enhance efficiency, 
Mamba integrates a hardware-optimized parallel processing algorithm.
In Mamba, the input and output maintain the same shape, denoted 
R𝐵×𝑊 ×𝑆 → R𝐵×𝑊 ×𝑆 , where 𝐵 represents the batch size, 𝑊  the window 
size, and 𝑆 the number of sensors. The input first undergoes linear 
projections to generate vectors 𝒙 and 𝒛 with size E. A one-dimensional 
convolution, followed by a SiLU activation [34], is applied to 𝒙, result-
ing in 𝒙′. The matrix 𝐴 is initialized as a structured state space, while 
𝐵 and 𝐶 are derived from 𝒙′ through linear transformations. The size 
of the step 𝛥 is calculated using a Softplus function applied to a combi-
nation of parameters and a transmitted transformation of 𝒙′, making 𝐵, 
𝐶, and 𝛥 adaptable to the input sequence. Although 𝐴 is not directly 
data dependent, the transformation ̄𝐴 = exp( 𝛥𝐴) introduces a degree 
of adaptability. This design enables Mamba to dynamically adjust its 
behavior based on the sequence context, establishing it as an input-
dependent selective model. Although the original Mamba architecture 
was designed for sequence modeling tasks with per-timestep outputs, 
we adapt it for scalar regression by introducing additional modules 
tailored for RUL prediction, as described in Section 3.3. This includes 
a Transformer-based head that enables both temporal aggregation and 
selective attention over the sequence dimension.

through the Mamba Encoder, we implement an additional Transformer 
block [20] that serves two purposes: (1) aggregating temporal features

3.4. Self-supervised learning
𝐿𝑡𝑜𝑡𝑎𝑙 = 𝐿𝑇 𝑒𝑚𝑝𝑜𝑟𝑎𝑙 + 𝐿𝑁−𝑡𝑢𝑝𝑙𝑒𝑡 + 𝐿𝑃 𝑠𝑒𝑢𝑑𝑜−𝑙𝑎𝑏𝑒𝑙 (10)
As mentioned in Section 3.2.2, the Mamba model used as the 
encoder outputs the same shape as the input, R𝐵×𝑊 ×𝑆 → R𝐵×𝑊 ×𝑆 . 
To extract high-quality features, as illustrated in Fig.  3, we employ the 
following three loss functions: temporal loss, N-tuplet loss, and pseudo-
label loss. The first two losses are designed to incorporate sequential 
patterns into the embeddings and do not involve any form of label 
information, directly or indirectly. The last loss assigns pseudo-labels 
to guide the health state of each data segment within the sequence.
3.4.1. Temporal ordering loss
In the context of RUL data, values evolve over time, reflecting pro-
gressive equipment degradation. To capture these temporal dynamics, 
we employ the temporal ordering loss (temporal loss), inspired by the 
method in [35], which encourages the model to distinguish between 
correct and incorrect sequential orders. In each batch, we construct a 
total of eight triplets: six that are correctly ordered and two that are 
intentionally disordered. These triplets are formed by sampling three 
embeddings from within a temporal window to reflect both short-range 
and long-range dependencies. For example, temporal triplets such as 
[i − 1, i, i + 1] and [i − 2, i, i + 3] preserve the correct temporal order 
and are labeled as ordered, while permutations such as [i + 2, i, i + 1] 
violate the natural sequence and are labeled as disordered. This process 
is illustrated in Fig.  4(a), which provides a visual overview of how 
ordered and disordered triplets are constructed. This results in a triplet 
tensor of shape R8×3×𝐿×𝐷 and corresponding binary labels of shape R8. 
A binary classifier determines whether each triplet maintains temporal 
order, and the Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss is used to optimize the 
model: 
Temp = (1 − 𝑜𝑛) log(1 − ̂ 𝑜𝑛) + 𝑜𝑛 log( ̂ 𝑜𝑛) (11)
where n refers to the index of the sample, 𝑜𝑛 ∈ {0 , 1} is a ground-
truth label indicating whether the triplet is in the correct temporal 
order (1 for ordered, 0 for disordered), while ̂ 𝑜𝑛 ∈ {0 , 1} is the 
predicted probability (output of a sigmoid layer) that the triplet is 
correctly ordered. This approach ensures that the embeddings reflect 
the temporal order of the sequences, enhancing the temporal accuracy 
and relevance of the features extracted by the Mamba encoder.
3.4.2. N-tuplet loss
The N-tuplet loss, another loss function used to capture sequential 
dependencies, enhances the model’s ability to discriminate between 
multiple data segments by comparing a positive example against mul-
tiple negative examples within a batch [36]. As illustrated in Fig. 
4(b), this approach aims to improve the discriminative ability of the 
embeddings and enhance model performance in distinguishing between

3.4.3. Pseudo-label loss
To enable training in the absence of explicit labels, we adopt a struc-
tured pseudo-labeling approach that leverages characteristic patterns 
in the sensor signals. The process consists of seven systematic steps, as 
formally described in Algorithm 2.
In Steps 1–3 (illustrated in Fig.  5), non-informative sensors — those 
exhibiting minimal variation or lacking degradation trends — are ex-
cluded. The remaining signals are normalized and then clustered using 
k-means  [37] (𝑘 = 3 ) to segment each sensor’s trajectory into three 
distinct behavioral groups. The average value (centroid) of each cluster 
is then computed to facilitate boundary selection. We adopted k-means 
clustering for auxiliary pseudo-label generation owing to its simplicity, 
computational efficiency, and practical effectiveness in distinguishing 
sensor signal patterns.
Steps 4–6 (illustrated in Fig.  6) involve identifying two decision 
thresholds based on the computed cluster centroids. The first threshold 
(𝑏1) is derived from the boundary between clusters 2 and 3, and the 
second threshold (𝑏2) is computed as the midpoint between the sensor’s 
minimum value and 𝑏1. These thresholds are used to assign stage-wise 
pseudo-labels (Label 1, 2, or 3) to each (window, sensor) instance.
Finally, in Step 7, a neural classifier is trained using cross-entropy 
loss to predict these pseudo-labels, guiding the model to learn a struc-
tured representation of sensor degradation patterns. The overall proce-
dure is summarized in Algorithm 2.

Piece-wise linear degradation
In the C-MAPSS dataset, sensor values remain constant until a cer-

reflects the piece-wise linear degradation patterns observed in the 
dataset shown in Fig.  7.
Sliding window
To capture temporal dependencies in the time-series data of the C-
MAPSS dataset, we apply a sliding window approach. This allows the 
model to learn from multiple time points rather than a single time 
point. The sliding stride is set to 1, and each window contains data 
samples from the same engine unit to maintain consistency.
Sensor selection
We utilized all 21 sensors from the C-MAPSS dataset, as MambAtt 
demonstrated strong performance without requiring a separate sensor 
selection process. However, for efficient clustering in the pseudo-label 
Loss, we removed sensors that were either constant or irrelevant. See 
3.4.3 for details.

4.2. CALCE dataset
To assess the generalization capability of our model, we addi-
tionally performed experiments using the publicly well-known CALCE 
Lithium-ion (Li-ion) battery dataset provided by the University of Mary-
land [39]. This dataset, which differs from the C-MAPSS aircraft engine 
dataset in domain characteristics, includes four battery models: CS2_35, 
CS2_36, CS2_37, and CS2_38. These batteries were tested at a constant 
temperature of 1 ◦C. We treated each of the four battery datasets 
as a distinct engine type and performed RUL prediction accordingly. 
The CALCE dataset, typical of long-life batteries in battery life re-
search [24], differs from the C-MAPSS dataset in that it generally 
contains batteries with over 800 cycles on average. Each battery un-
dergoes a sequence of three operations: charging, discharging, and 
impedance measurements.
4.2.1. RUL calculation for the CALCE dataset
In Li-ion battery RUL prediction, capacity value is broadly regarded 
as the health indicator of a battery to quantify degradation. A battery 
reaches its End Of Life (EOL) threshold when its capacity is reduced 
to seventy or eighty percent of the initial value [40]. RUL is defined 
as the amount of time remaining before system health falls below a 
pre-determined failure threshold.
Similar to previous studies [40–42] that performed RUL prediction 
using the CALCE battery dataset, our approach forecasts future capacity 
values to enable indirect inference of remaining useful life based on 
degradation trends.
4.2.2. CALCE dataset pre-processing
Following the training data setup used in [40,41], we applied
Normalization and Sliding window (with a window size of 64 and 
a stride of 1) to the input capacity sequences, as described earlier. Raw 
input data often contains much noise, particularly during charge/dis-
charge regeneration, which has a detrimental effect on the accuracy 
of predictions [40]. We additionally applied a dropout-based outlier 
removal process.
Dropout outlier
The input sequence is divided into non-overlapping segments of 
fixed size (bins), and within each segment, data points that fall outside 
two standard deviations from the mean are considered outliers and 
excluded. The remaining indices are retained and used as valid input 
for training.

5.1. Experimental settings
The experimental setup followed the configuration presented in 
Table  2. Following the experimental settings outlined in [22], we 
implement multiple random seeds to ensure robustness and prevent 
the model from converging to a local minimum. The model is trained 
50 or 5 times on each of the four sub-datasets using different random 
seeds. Subsequently, the model exhibiting the lowest RMSE on the 
validation set is selected and evaluated on the test set. In the few-
shot learning scenario, for C-MAPSS dataset, a small number of samples 
are randomly selected to reflect real-world conditions. One of these 
is further designated as a validation sample to determine the optimal 
training epoch. For the CALCE dataset, the battery CS2_35 is used 
as the one-shot test instance. In the two-shot setting, we conducted 
additional experiments using various combinations of labeled samples 
to assess the consistency and generalizability of the model. Since the 
CALCE dataset does not exhibit a clear degradation trend suitable for 
pseudo-labeling, we pre-trained the model using only the temporal and 
N-tuplet losses.
For validation purposes in C-MAPSS dataset experiments, 80% of 
the engines are designated as the training set, while the remaining 
20% are used as the validation set. For the CALCE battery dataset, 
we adopted a leave-one-out evaluation strategy. In each iteration, one 
battery was designated as the test sample, while the others were used 
for training. This procedure was repeated five times, and the final 
results were reported as the average performance across all batteries.

6. Conclusion
In this study, we proposed Mamba-Attention (MambAtt) model, 
which overcomes the limitations of traditional RNNs and attention-
based models, such as vanishing gradients, inefficiencies in long se-
quence processing, and high computational complexity. By integrating 
a selective state space model with attention mechanisms, MambAtt 
achieved state-of-the-art performance in RUL prediction tasks while 
improving training speed. We also explored the use of self-supervised 
learning (SSL) and few-shot learning approaches. With SSL techniques: 
temporal triplet loss, N-tuplet loss, and pseudo-label loss, the model 
effectively generated accurate RUL predictions with minimal labeled 
data. Few-shot learning further demonstrated the framework’s robust-
ness, though challenges remain, particularly in performance variability 
based on the chosen few-shot labels. Future research should focus 
on reducing this variability to enhance the stability and reliability of 
few-shot learning in RUL prediction.
